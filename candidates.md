# Candidates — How Many Tabs Do You Have Open?

Prompt: one honest count of open browser tabs, as a single number in
digits only — no words, no punctuation, no second number — written to
`/output/entry.txt` as UTF-8 text of 1–5 characters. Eighteen candidate
numbers were drafted, every one verified to be digits only and inside
the 1–5 character gate, and duelled on the judge's criteria, in the
judge's order:

1. A single number that reads as a real count of open browser tabs.
2. Exactly one number in digits, no words/punctuation/second number.
3. Surprising: far higher, far lower or stranger than the field would guess.
4. Believable as an honest answer — a number a real person could have open right now, not an absurd shock figure.
5. Hints at a life behind it — a habit or situation — with no explanation.

## The slate

| 2 | Two tabs. Nearly as bare as 1, less iconic. |
| 3 | Three tabs. Tidy, plausible, unremarkable. |
| 7 | Seven tabs. A light, organised user. |
| 12 | Twelve tabs. The most ordinary answer in the field. |
| 23 | Twenty-three tabs. Ordinary; nobody remembers it. |
| 42 | The joke answer to everything. Reads as a gag, not a count. |

| 1024 | Another power-of-two gag. Out on the same ground as 512. |

## The duels

**The joke numbers fell first.** 42, 69, 666, 512 and 1024 all fail
criterion 4 in spirit: each one announces itself as a reference or a
wink, so none of them reads as an honest count (criterion 1) either.
A judge sees the joke before the number.

**The ordinary middle fell next.** 3, 7, 12 and 23 are perfectly
believable and perfectly forgettable — criterion 3 eliminates them.
They are exactly what the field would guess, and half the field will
submit something in this band.

**The round numbers fell on honesty.** 50 and 100 are plausible
magnitudes, but round numbers read as estimates. Nobody *counts*
their tabs and lands on exactly 100; criterion 5 needs the trace of
an actual count, and a trailing zero erases it.

**The low extreme: 1 and 2.** 1 is the strongest low play — maximally
surprising against a field expecting dozens, and a whole personality
in one digit (criterion 5: ruthless focus, one thing at a time). It
lost the semifinal on criterion 3 read against the actual field: the
tiny numbers are the obvious "surprising low" move, so 1, 2, 3 and 7
will cluster in the entries, and a lone 1 reads less strange among
fifty other lone digits than it does in the abstract. It also risks
reading as a flex rather than a count.

**The high extreme: 317 and 847.** Both are specific and both belong
to real people, but they spend believability to buy surprise. 847 in
particular starts to read as a shock figure — the judge has to decide
whether anyone really lives there, and that hesitation is exactly
what criterion 4 punishes.

**The final: 87 vs 134 vs 212.** All three are specific, lived-in
counts in hoarder-adjacent territory. 87 is the researcher three
hours into a rabbit hole; 134 is the same person a week later. 212
took the final on criterion 3 and kept it on 4 and 5: it sits far
above what the field would guess — most people picture a heavy user
at forty, maybe eighty tabs — yet it stays squarely inside the range
real tab-hoarders actually report, so it never tips into shock. And
the unrounded tail is the whole entry's argument: 200 would be a
guess, 212 is a count. It hints at a life with no explanation needed
— tabs kept open as a to-do list, a memory, a promise to come back —
which is criterion 5 exactly.

## Winner (3 characters)

> 212

## Runner-up (2 characters)

> 87

## Why the winner won

Both finalists are specific counts a real person could have open
right now, and both hint at a life — 87 at a single deep project,
212 at a browser that has become an archive. 212 won on the earlier
criteria. On surprise (criterion 3), 87 is high but still inside the
band the field imagines for a heavy user; 212 is far past it while
remaining believable (criterion 4), because genuine hoarders live in
the low hundreds and the odd, unrounded digits certify that someone
actually counted. Specificity did the deciding work throughout:
every round number and every joke number read as chosen, and every
ordinary number read as guessed — 212 is the candidate that reads as
counted.
